# Character and background rules (generic)

These are the generic parts of the rules we used. Details that belong to one video (a particular culture, a particular narrator design) are left out.

## The narrator character

- One recurring character who tells the story, drawn once and reused. Good for: asides, questions, "but" and "yet" twists, introducing a person or an idea, the line before a story scene, the closing.
- On screen at least once a minute; about 30 to 40 percent of a video. Beats are short: a few seconds, not more than about 12 s in one go, and not closer together than about 8 s.
- Never on a plain colour wall. Put the narrator (a) over the live map or scene with only a soft halo around the outline (the background stays sharp), (b) beside a portrait card with a name written on it, a chart, an idea card or a fact card, or (c) inside a drawn place (a museum room, ruins, a workshop).
- Size: about 55 to 60 percent of the frame height for a beat, 40 to 50 percent when popping over a map.
- Acting: a pose change on the key word (point, present, shrug), a surprised face on the twist, the mouth moving only while speaking and closed in pauses. A generic mouth flap is enough.
- A small version of the narrator can ride a progress or timeline bar and jump when the story jumps in time. Never the big and the small narrator at the same moment.

## Story scenes

- A cartoon scene for a moment with two sides: a ruler and an adviser, an envoy and a local, a deal, a refusal, a betrayal. Or an everyday-life vignette (market, village, street) with no dialogue. About one a minute, 6 to 20 seconds each.
- Two main characters, plus 2 to 6 small background people so the world feels alive; never one lone character. Crowds and armies: 6 to 12 detailed figures in front, repeated figures behind.
- Characters about 6 heads tall, thin uniform dark outline (about 2 px at 1080p), flat colour plus one darker shade layer for folds, dot eyes with expressive brows, simple 4-finger hands that read as gestures. Main characters 55 to 80 percent of the frame height, extras 10 to 25 percent.
- Costume exact to the culture, period and rank (crowns, hats, helmets, patterned robes, sashes, armour, tools). Pick the cast from the cultures the narration names.
- Acting: a pose swap on every spoken line (every 1 to 3 seconds), mouths while talking, short walks in and out (about 2 s), shock faces on twists. The camera stays still or pushes in very slowly. Scenes start and end with a hard cut.
- Dialogue lines are short (8 words or fewer), voiced by a different voice from the narrator, and written on screen above the speaker. The narrator delivers the punchline after the scene.
- Humour comes from situations and reactions, never from a culture or group. Violence is shown with smoke, a cross, greying; no gore.

## Backgrounds and sets

- Never a plain background. A set has three layers: foreground props (pots, baskets, rocks, carpets), characters and architecture, far hills, sky and clouds. 5 to 12 distinct props per scene.
- Lighting by mood: bright day by default, sunset orange and smoke for battles and aftermath, dark interior for defeat or death, red-orange for a city burning.
- Background motion is small (smoke, flags, sun rays); the life comes from the characters.
- Right period only: ancient scenes use period paintings or drawn animation, never modern photos.

## Maps

- A map is a stage that stays 8 to 25 seconds while things happen on it. Every map has at least one moving element (camera, growing region, tokens, route traffic). A map with static labels is a still photo in disguise.
- Camera always easing, never stopping or resetting; 3 to 6 s eased moves with a slow drift between.
- Regions fill with bright see-through colour (55 to 75 percent), a thin white edge, regions grow from a point into their outline.
- Armies as clusters of 6 to 9 heads with a dashed trail; leader heads 4 to 6 percent of the frame height; round emblem badges; red pins that pop on the word; counters that roll for numbers; thin letter-spaced labels typed in; icons for ideas (scrolls, money bags, books).
- 6 to 12 map events a minute. No end zoom-out.
- Disputed borders: pick one named source for the borders, use it everywhere, and say which in the description.

## Photos and paintings

- Pop in as a card at 40 to 50 percent of the width, enlarge to full screen for 3 to 5 seconds with a slow push, then shrink or cut back. A small card left sitting on a map for long is the habit to avoid (it was 20 percent of our first video; the reference had 3).
- A low-resolution source: about 70 percent size over a blurred copy of itself.
- An object you can still visit: 2 to 3 seconds of real photos, then the narrator inside a drawn version of the place.

## What the narration says, what the screen does

| The narration is about | Show |
|---|---|
| a kingdom growing, borders, routes, a war front | map, region grows, army tokens, camera eases to the event word |
| what a ruler decided, a deal, a refusal | dialogue story scene, then the narrator delivers the punchline |
| what something meant for ordinary people | everyday-life story scene |
| a person or an idea introduced | narrator beside a portrait card with the name written on it |
| an object, building or artwork you can see today | full-screen photos 2 to 3 s each, then the narrator in a drawn place |
| an ironic twist, "but", "yet" | the picture greys out or the narrator pops in, a fact card |
| a number | a counter rolling on the map or scene |
| a date or era change | the timeline bar rolls to the year |
| a question to the viewer | narrator turns to the camera, curious face |

## Respect

Positive and neutral tone about people and places. No mocking a group, no gore, no framing a place only by its poverty or its wars. Hard facts can be told as history, in the past tense, tied to a period.
